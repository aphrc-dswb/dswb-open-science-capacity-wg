## Speaker - Dr Caleb Kibet

### Speaker bio
Dr. Caleb Kibet is a bioinformatics researcher, lecturer, open science advocate, and mentor with a PhD in Bioinformatics from Rhodes University. He currently serves as a Data Scientist at the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative (IAVI), Nairobi office, where his work spans BCR repertoire analysis, single-cell genomics, and bioinformatics pipeline development supporting germline-targeting HIV vaccine clinical trials.

Caleb is an adjunct lecturer at Pwani University and is a former Mozilla Open Science Fellow. He serves on the board of the Open Bioinformatics Foundation; is the founder of OpenScienceKE, an initiative promoting open approaches to bioinformatics research across Kenya, and Jenomu, a bioinformatics marketplace. He is passionate about open science and reproducible research, and continually seeks opportunities to spread the open science message within the bioinformatics community in Africa.

## Notes

   * **What is a health data system:**
       * Not all health data system is the same, Some require openness, others require a high level of closeness.
       * Aggregate indicators: 
           * Examples: National HIV prevalence • Under-5 mortality • disease incidence
           * Access level: Fully open
       * De-identified survey data
           * Examples: DHS/MICS microdata • individual-level, stripped of direct identifiers
           * Access level: Controlled open
       * Pseudonymised clinical
           * Examples: Electronic medical records • patient IDs replaced • longitudinal treatment data records
           * Access level: TRE only
       * Identifiable clinical data
           * Examples: Patient name + diagnosis + treatment history - directly linkable
           * Access level: Highly restricted
       * Genomic / biological data
           * Examples: Whole genome sequences. BCR repertoires • pathogen isolates
           * Access level: Governed access
       * Community-sensitive aggregates
           * Examples: HIV prevalence by village, sexual behaviour by district; small groups re-identify
           * Access level: Re-identifiable risk
       * Note:  'aggregate' program data can re-identify small communities. Aggregate * safe.
   * The goal is to have FAIR data, not just open. 
   * **Systems within Africa:**
       * DHIS2
       * OpenMRS
       * IDSR
       * H3Africa
       * eLwazi / DS-I Africa
       * Africa CDC
       * The question with Africa is not if the systems or data exsits, they do, the concern is who has the data. 
   * **Gaps that make openness complicated:**
       * Civil registration (CRVS): most African deaths go unregistered. Cause of death is largely absent from the health data ecosystem, creating systematic blind spots
       * ﻿﻿Interoperability: DHIS2, OpenMRS, LMIS, and lab systems are siloed. A patient journey crosses all four and is visible in none ofthem
       * ﻿﻿Infrastructure: power and internet gaps mean foreign-hosted systems fill the void, creating data dependency as a side-effect of a connectivity problem
       * ﻿﻿Data quality: DHIS2 reporting completeness runs 60-80% in many countries, and gaps cluster in highest-burden areas
       * ﻿﻿Workforce: trained health informaticians leave for better-paid NGO and Global North roles (brain drain)
       * ﻿﻿Legal fragmentation: 54 countries with 54 data protection frameworks. Funder mandates and national laws conflict with no mechanism to resolve it
   * **Africa is a data generator, just not a consumer:**
       * ﻿﻿African genomic diversity is irreplaceable for HIV vaccine design. A germline-targeting vaccine, like VRC01-class, cannot be designed without knowing the actual germline landscape in African populations
       * ﻿﻿COVID-19 variant surveillance (Omicron, Beta) was only possible because African labs shared sequences openly
       * ﻿﻿PEPFAR HIV program data covers 25+ African countries. When that system was threatened, millions of patient records and ongoing care were at immediate risk
   * **IAVI DataSpace**
       * Context: Protocol C HIV study • 613 participants • East \& Southern Africa • 2006-2017. Sequencing by Welcome Sanger.
           * Tier 1 - Freely available
           * Tier 2 - Controlled access
           * Tier 3 — Future (federated)
       * Design: as open as possible, as closed as necessary — access level set by re-identification risk, not administrative default
   * The existing legal frameworks are well-intentioned but can sometimes cause friction between different countries, as input is not always gotten from all countries when drafting these. For example, the EU data policy has some African countries 
   * **Guardrails:**
       * Not having adequate guardrails in place creates spaces for "Helicopter Research".
       * There is the desire to be opeb, but the openness should create impact, instead of bans, restrictions or being sidelined because of the data being shared.
       * "Open" data that flows in one direction is not 'open', it is extraction. 
   * **Risks of unrestricted data openness in African health:**
       * Extraction without reciprocity
       * Privacy and re-identification
       * Political and geopolitical leverage
       * Epistemic colonialism
   * The data should also be FAIR. Open data does not mean it is FAIR, it has to be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable. 
   * FAIR explicitly perserves privacy. It follows the concept of "As open as possible, as closed as necessary."
   * **Trusted Research Environments:**
       * These have five "Safes": Safe people, safe projects, safe settings, safe data, safe outputs.
       * A TRE is not a technical barrier — it is a governance mechanism that makes collaboration possible under conditions that protect sovereignty.
   * The goal is not openness for its own sake. The goal is impact that stays - in communities, in clinics, in African institutions.
